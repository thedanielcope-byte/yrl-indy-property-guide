Buying a Home With an HOA in Central Indiana

Homeowners associations are everywhere in the Indianapolis suburbs. Here is what to understand about dues, rules, and documents before you buy.

Buying a Home Central Indiana By Daniel Cope

✔ Reviewed by Janet Giles-Schultz, Principal Broker · MIBOR member · Updated August 2026

Buying a Home August 2026 By Daniel Cope, Real Estate Broker — Your Realty Link ~8 min read

If you are house hunting in the Central Indiana suburbs, there is a very good chance the home you fall in love with belongs to a homeowners association. From the newer neighborhoods in Fishers and Westfield to the growing communities in Brownsburg, Whitestown, and McCordsville, HOAs are simply part of suburban life here. They are not something to fear, but they are something to understand before you sign. This guide walks through what an HOA is, what your dues pay for, the rules that come with the territory, and the documents you should read closely before you commit.

What an HOA Actually Is

A homeowners association, or HOA, is a private organization that manages and governs a residential community. When you buy in an HOA neighborhood, you automatically become a member, and membership is not optional. The association is typically run by a volunteer board of homeowners, often with help from a professional management company, and it is funded by the dues that every owner pays. Its job is to maintain shared spaces, enforce community standards, and protect property values across the neighborhood.

Here in Central Indiana, HOAs show up most often in newer subdivisions, planned communities, condos, and townhome developments. If you are drawn to the amenity-rich neighborhoods going up around Westfield, Noblesville, and Whitestown, an HOA is almost a given. Buyers exploring the many new construction communities in the Indianapolis suburbs should expect one, since builders establish the association as part of developing the neighborhood. Older, established Indianapolis neighborhoods are less likely to have a mandatory HOA, though some have voluntary neighborhood groups you can choose to join.

What Your HOA Dues Typically Cover

HOA dues vary widely from one community to the next, and they can be billed monthly, quarterly, or annually. A single-family neighborhood with a shared entrance and a couple of common areas will sit at the lower end. A community with a pool, clubhouse, fitness center, and miles of walking trails will cost more, because those amenities take real money to maintain. Condo and townhome associations tend to charge the most, because their dues often cover exterior building maintenance that a single-family owner would handle on their own.

  • Common area upkeep: mowing, mulching, and maintaining the shared green spaces, islands, and entrances.
  • Amenities: the pool, clubhouse, playground, trails, and fitness areas many Hamilton County communities are known for.
  • Stormwater and retention ponds: the drainage infrastructure that keeps a neighborhood functioning is often an HOA responsibility.
  • Neighborhood entrance and signage: lighting, landscaping, and seasonal decor at the community entrance.
  • Exterior maintenance and trash: in many condo and townhome associations, roofs, siding, and trash service are bundled in.

It is worth asking exactly what your dues include, because the answer shapes your true monthly cost of ownership. Two homes with the same price can carry very different HOA obligations, and that difference belongs in your budget from day one rather than as a surprise after you move in.

The Rules and Covenants You Are Agreeing To

Every HOA operates under a set of governing documents, and the most important of these are the Covenants, Conditions, and Restrictions, usually shortened to CC and Rs. These are recorded rules that run with the land, which means they apply to you the moment you take ownership. They exist to keep the neighborhood consistent and protect resale value, but they can also limit things you might assume are entirely your call.

  • Exterior appearance: paint colors, roofing materials, and landscaping choices often require approval.
  • Fences and structures: fence height and style, sheds, and additions frequently need board sign-off.
  • Parking: rules on street parking, boats, RVs, and commercial vehicles are common.
  • Leasing and rentals: some associations cap how many homes can be rented, which matters if you ever want to lease yours.
  • Everyday details: basketball hoops, satellite dishes, flags, and even holiday decorations can be regulated.

None of this is necessarily bad. The same rules that stop your neighbor from parking a boat on the lawn also protect your view and your value. The point is simply to read the CC and Rs before closing so nothing surprises you. If a particular restriction would be a dealbreaker for how you plan to live, it is far better to learn that now. The seller disclosure may hint at association obligations, but the governing documents are where the real detail lives.

Read the HOA Documents During Your Contingency Window

This is the single most important step, and it is the one buyers most often rush. When you are under contract, you have a window to investigate the home before your commitment becomes final. That same window is your chance to review the HOA's documents. Use your contingency period to request and actually read the association's financials, not just the rulebook.

Pay attention to the budget and, especially, the reserve fund. Reserves are the savings an association sets aside for big-ticket repairs like repaving roads or replacing a pool. An underfunded reserve is a warning sign, because it often means a special assessment is coming, and that is a bill every owner has to pay. Read the recent meeting minutes for talk of lawsuits, major projects, or dues increases, and ask directly whether any special assessments are planned.

Line this review up alongside your home inspection. While your inspector evaluates the physical condition of the house, you evaluate the financial and legal health of the association. Both protect you, and both belong inside your investigation window. If the documents are hard to get or the numbers look thin, that itself tells you something important before you are locked in.

The Pros and Cons of HOA Living

HOA living is a genuine trade-off, and reasonable buyers land on both sides of it. On the positive side, associations keep neighborhoods looking sharp, maintain amenities you would never fund alone, and give you a layer of protection against a neighbor's choices dragging down your value. For busy families and first-time buyers, the predictability can be a real comfort.

The flip side is cost and control. Dues are a permanent line item that can rise over time, special assessments can arrive unexpectedly, and the rules mean you do not have total freedom over your own property. Enforcement can also feel heavy-handed in some communities. Whether that trade is worth it depends entirely on you, which is why matching the neighborhood to your lifestyle matters as much as matching the house to your budget.

One honest way to decide is to picture your daily life in the neighborhood five years from now. If you value low-maintenance living, community amenities, and a tidy, predictable street, the dues may feel like money well spent. If you dream of a workshop in the yard, a boat in the driveway, or full freedom to paint the house any color you like, a lighter-touch neighborhood may suit you better. Neither answer is wrong, and there is no shortage of both kinds of homes across Central Indiana.

Questions to Ask Before You Buy

Before you write an offer on an HOA home, get clear answers to a short list of questions. A good buyer's agent will help you gather these and read between the lines of the answers.

  • What are the dues, and how often do they rise? Ask for a few years of history if you can get it.
  • How healthy are the reserves? A well-funded reserve lowers your risk of a surprise assessment.
  • Are any special assessments planned? This is the question that saves buyers the most heartache.
  • What does the leasing policy allow? This is critical if you might rent the home someday.
  • Are there restrictions I would find frustrating? Fences, parking, and exterior changes top the list.

Frequently Asked Questions

Are HOAs mandatory in Central Indiana suburbs?

In most newer subdivisions and planned communities, yes. When an HOA governs the neighborhood, membership and dues are automatic and not optional. Many older Indianapolis neighborhoods have no mandatory HOA, so it varies by community.

Can an HOA really tell me what to do with my own home?

Within the limits of its recorded covenants, yes. HOAs can regulate things like exterior paint, fences, parking, and rentals. That is why reading the CC and Rs before you close is so important.

What is an HOA special assessment?

It is an extra charge beyond regular dues, usually to fund a large repair or cover a shortfall. Reviewing the association's reserves and meeting minutes before buying helps you gauge how likely one is.
