Buying a Rural Home in Central Indiana: Wells, Septic & Radon

Acreage and country homes come with a few systems city buyers never think about. Here's what to check before you buy in rural Central Indiana.

Buying a Home Rural & Acreage By Daniel Cope

✔ Reviewed by Janet Giles-Schultz, Principal Broker · MIBOR member · Updated August 2026

Buying a Home August 2026 By Daniel Cope, Real Estate Broker — Your Realty Link ~8 min read

Buying a rural home in Central Indiana can be one of the most rewarding moves you'll ever make — more land, more quiet, more room to breathe just a short drive from the metro. But a country property runs on systems a subdivision buyer never has to think about, and the disclosures and inspections look different too. Before you fall for the wraparound porch and the ten acres behind it, our team wants you to understand what actually needs checking so you buy with your eyes open.

Well Water: Test It, Don't Assume It

Most rural Central Indiana homes get their water from a private well rather than a municipal line, which means water quality and supply are the buyer's responsibility, not a utility's. Before you close, have the water tested for bacteria (coliform and E. coli), nitrates, and — depending on the area — hardness, iron, and other minerals. A separate flow-rate check confirms the well actually produces enough water for a full household.

Also ask how old the well and pump are, how deep the well is, and when it was last serviced. A shallow or aging well isn't a dealbreaker, but it changes your budget and your expectations. Our team helps buyers line up the right well and water tests and read the results — the seller's disclosure is a starting point, not the final word.

Septic Systems: Inspect and Pump

Without city sewer, rural homes rely on a private septic system — a tank and a drain (leach) field that quietly does a big job. A failing septic system is expensive to replace, so a dedicated septic inspection (not just a general home inspection) is worth every dollar. A good inspector locates the tank, checks its condition, and evaluates whether the drain field is working.

Ask when the tank was last pumped (every few years is typical), how many bedrooms the system was permitted for, and whether the county health department has records on file. If you're planning to add bedrooms later, permitted capacity matters. This goes beyond a standard home inspection, which is exactly why rural buyers need an agent who knows to order it.

Radon: Indiana Is a High-Radon State

Radon is a naturally occurring radioactive gas that seeps up from the soil, and much of Indiana sits in the EPA's highest-risk zone. It's odorless and invisible, and it's the kind of thing you simply test for rather than guess about — rural or not. A radon test during your inspection period tells you where the home stands.

The good news: elevated radon is very fixable. A mitigation system (essentially a vent pipe and fan that pulls the gas out from under the home) is a well-understood, reasonably priced fix, and it's a normal thing to negotiate for in the purchase agreement. The point isn't to fear it — it's to test, know the number, and address it if needed.

Access, Easements, Propane, and Floodplain

Country properties come with a few more questions worth answering up front:

  • Road access & easements. Is the driveway on a public road, or do you cross a neighbor's land on a shared or private easement? Confirm access is legally recorded — don't rely on a handshake.
  • Propane & heating. Many rural homes heat with propane or fuel oil rather than natural gas. Ask whether the tank is owned or leased, and factor delivery into your budget.
  • Floodplain. Land near creeks and rivers can sit in a FEMA flood zone, which affects insurance and lending. It's a quick thing to check before you're deep into a deal.
  • Outbuildings & zoning. Barns, pole buildings, and agricultural use can carry their own rules. If you have plans for the land, confirm the zoning allows them.

Frequently Asked Questions

Do I need a separate septic inspection when buying a rural home?

Yes. A standard home inspection doesn't fully evaluate a septic system. A dedicated septic inspection locates the tank, checks its condition, and assesses the drain field — well worth it, since replacing a failed system is expensive.

Should I test the well water before buying?

Absolutely. On a private well, water quality and supply are the buyer's responsibility. Test for bacteria and nitrates and check the flow rate during your inspection period so you know exactly what you're buying.

Is radon a big deal in Indiana?

Much of Indiana is in the EPA's highest-risk radon zone, so testing is smart on any home. Elevated radon is common and very fixable with a reasonably priced mitigation system, which you can negotiate for in the purchase agreement.

What else is different about buying an acreage property?

Beyond wells, septic, and radon, confirm legal road access and easements, whether propane tanks are owned or leased, floodplain status, and that zoning allows your plans for any outbuildings or land use.
