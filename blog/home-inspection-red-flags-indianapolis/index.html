Home Inspection Red Flags Every Indy Buyer Should Know

From century-old bungalows to brand-new builds, Central Indiana homes hide different problems. Learn which inspection findings are routine and which deserve a hard second look.

Buying a Home Central Indiana By Daniel Cope

✔ Reviewed by Janet Giles-Schultz, Principal Broker · MIBOR member · Updated August 2026

Buying a Home August 2026 By Daniel Cope, Real Estate Broker — Your Realty Link ~8 min read

Picture yourself in the driveway of a 1920s bungalow in Irvington, keys almost in hand, when the inspector waves you toward the basement. This is the moment a purchase gets real. A home inspection is your one scheduled chance to look past fresh paint and staging and understand what you are actually buying. In Central Indiana, where the housing stock ranges from century-old near-north-side homes to brand-new builds in Hancock County, knowing which findings are routine and which are true red flags can save you thousands of dollars and a lot of heartache. Here is how to read your inspection like a pro.

What a Home Inspection Actually Covers

A general home inspection is a top-to-bottom visual review of the home's major systems and structure — roof, foundation, framing, electrical, plumbing, heating and cooling, insulation, drainage, and the visible condition of walls, floors, windows, and doors. The inspector is not tearing open walls; they report on what can be seen and safely accessed.

You will receive a written report, usually with photos, sorting findings by urgency. Most reports run long and list dozens of items. That length is normal and does not mean the home is a lemon — even a solid house generates a long list because a good inspector documents everything, right down to a loose railing or a worn washer.

Plan to attend if you can, at least for the final walkthrough. Standing in the crawl space with the inspector teaches you more than any PDF ever will. Bring your questions and ask them to point out what is urgent, what is routine, and what they would keep an eye on.

Red Flags That Matter Most in Indy-Area Homes

Every home has quirks, but a handful of issues carry outsized weight in Central Indiana because of our soil, our weather, and our older neighborhoods. These are the findings worth slowing down for.

  • Foundation and settling: Indiana's clay-heavy soils expand and contract with moisture. Horizontal cracks, bowing basement walls, or doors that will not close can signal movement worth a structural engineer's opinion.
  • Roof age and condition: Ask how many years of life are left. A roof near the end of its span is a major near-term expense.
  • Aging furnace and HVAC: Our real winters and humid summers work these systems hard. An older furnace or air conditioner may run today but sit close to replacement.
  • Water in basements and crawl spaces: Standing water, chalky efflorescence, a musty smell, or a failed sump pump all point to drainage problems that invite mold and rot.
  • Older wiring: In historic pockets like Irvington and the near-north side, you may find knob-and-tube or ungrounded wiring, or an overloaded panel that needs updating for safety and insurance.
  • Sewer line issues: Older clay laterals and mature tree roots are a classic Indy combination. A separate sewer scope can reveal cracks or blockages before they become a costly dig.

Not every one of these kills a deal. But each deserves a closer look, often from a specialist, before you decide how to move forward. A scary line item on paper often turns into a manageable number once a pro takes a real look.

Normal Wear Versus a Real Problem

The art of reading an inspection is separating cosmetic, routine wear from structural or safety concerns. A dripping faucet, worn caulk, a few cracked driveway sections, or a handful of dead outlets are maintenance items — annoying, inexpensive, and expected in a lived-in home. A cracked heat exchanger, active water intrusion, or a foundation that is moving is a completely different category.

Compare the inspection against the seller's disclosure, the form where Indiana sellers report known defects. If the inspector finds an issue the seller marked as no problem, that is worth a direct conversation. It may be an honest oversight, or it may change how you approach the negotiation.

When in doubt, get a specialist quote. A roofer, HVAC technician, structural engineer, or plumber can turn a vague, alarming note into a firm dollar figure, which is exactly what you need to negotiate with confidence rather than fear.

Do Not Skip the Radon Test

Radon is an invisible, odorless radioactive gas that seeps up from soil and rock, and it is the one hazard many Indiana buyers overlook. Much of Indiana falls within the EPA's highest-risk radon zone, which makes testing a genuinely smart move here regardless of the neighborhood or the home's age.

A radon test is inexpensive relative to the peace of mind it buys, and it is usually ordered alongside the general inspection. If levels come back elevated, the fix is typically a mitigation system that vents the gas safely away from the home — a solvable and often negotiable problem. Confirm current guidance and action levels with the Indiana State Department of Health or a certified radon professional rather than relying on rules of thumb.

Do not assume a newer home is automatically safe. Radon depends on the ground beneath the house, not the year it was built. Test regardless, and you will know rather than guess.

Negotiating Repairs After the Inspection

In most Central Indiana purchase agreements, your power to act on the inspection comes from the inspection contingency. This clause gives you a defined window to review the report and respond — by requesting repairs, asking for a credit, renegotiating the price, or walking away if the two sides cannot agree.

Smart negotiating means picking your battles. Bundling every minor item tends to frustrate sellers and dilute your real ask. Focusing on health, safety, and big-ticket structural or mechanical findings lands far better. Your options generally include:

  • Seller makes the repairs: Completed with agreed contractors before closing.
  • Seller credit: A closing-cost credit so you control the repair after you own the home.
  • Price reduction: A lower price that reflects the work needed.
  • Walk away: Exercising the contingency to cancel and protect your earnest money if the issues are simply too big.

Should You Ever Waive an Inspection?

In competitive stretches, some buyers waive the inspection to make an offer stand out. Be careful. Waiving it means giving up your best window to discover expensive problems before you are legally committed. For most buyers, and especially first-timers, that risk is not worth it.

An as-is sale is a related but different situation. As-is means the seller will not make repairs, but in most cases you can still inspect for your own information and still keep the right to walk away based on what you find. Read your contract carefully, because the exact wording matters.

If you feel pressure to waive, talk to your agent about alternatives such as a shorter inspection window, an information-only inspection, or a cap on the repairs you will request. There is usually a middle path that keeps your offer strong without leaving you blind. If you are early in the process, our first-time home buyer guide for Indianapolis shows how inspections fit the bigger timeline, and because major findings can echo into the lender's valuation, it is worth understanding what hurts a home appraisal in Indiana too.

Read every inspection report by category, not by length. A two-page list of maintenance items is usually a healthy home, while one line about a moving foundation is the sentence that deserves your full attention.

Frequently Asked Questions

How long does a home inspection take in Indianapolis?

Most general inspections run a few hours depending on the size and age of the home. Plan to join for at least the final walkthrough so the inspector can show you the findings in person.

Is a home inspection required to buy a home in Indiana?

No, it is not legally required, and a cash buyer can waive it. But it is strongly recommended, because it is your main chance to find costly problems before you are committed.

Should I get a separate radon and sewer test?

Often yes. A general inspection does not always include radon testing or a sewer scope, and both catch expensive Indiana-specific problems. Ask your inspector which add-ons they offer.
