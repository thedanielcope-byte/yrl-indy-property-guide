What Kind of Property Survey Do You Need? A Central Indiana Buyer's Guide

Surveys are optional for buyers, but knowing your options can save money and prevent boundary headaches.

Buyer Tips Closing Process Central Indiana

✔ Reviewed by Janet Giles-Schultz, Principal Broker · MIBOR member · Updated August 2026

Buying a Home August 2026 By Daniel Cope, Real Estate Broker — Your Realty Link ~6 min read

Somewhere in the middle of buying a home, a survey question usually comes up, and many first-time buyers are not sure what it means or whether they need one. A survey helps confirm where a property's boundaries actually are and what sits inside them. In Indiana, a survey is typically optional for the buyer, but understanding your choices helps you decide where to spend. For the full picture, see our overview of the Indianapolis home buying process.

What a Survey Actually Tells You

A property survey identifies the legal boundaries of the parcel you are buying and shows how the home, driveway, fences, and other features sit relative to those lines. It can reveal encroachments, such as a neighbor's shed crossing the property line, or easements that give someone else the right to use part of the land.

For buyers, that information answers practical questions: exactly what am I buying, where can I build a fence or addition, and are there any surprises along the edges of the lot?

Two Common Survey Options

Buyers generally choose between two options, and the cost difference between them is significant. A staked or boundary survey is the more thorough and more expensive choice. A licensed surveyor physically measures the property and places stakes or markers at the corners, so you can see the actual boundary lines on the ground.

The less expensive option is often a survey location report, which is referenced in many Indiana purchase agreements. It shows the general location of improvements on the parcel but does not stake the corners with the same precision. Because one is far more detailed than the other, it is worth knowing which you are paying for.

When a Survey Is Worth It

A more detailed staked survey makes sense when you plan to build a fence or addition, when the lot is large or irregular, when there is any question about where a boundary falls, or when a neighbor's structure appears close to the line. Rural and acreage properties around Central Indiana often benefit from the extra clarity.

On a typical subdivision lot with clear, recent records and no plans to build near the edges, many buyers are comfortable with the lighter option. The right choice depends on the property and your plans for it.

How Surveys Fit Into Closing

Because a survey is usually elective for the buyer in Indiana, it is your decision whether to order one and which type to request. Your agent and closing team can point you to licensed local surveyors and help you weigh the cost against the peace of mind.

If you have any doubt about boundaries, it is far cheaper to resolve it before closing than to discover a problem afterward. A short conversation with your agent early in the process helps you budget for the right survey from the start.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is a survey required to buy a house in Indiana?

A survey is typically optional for the buyer rather than legally required. However, some lenders or title companies may request one, so confirm early with your closing team.

What is the difference between a staked survey and a survey location report?

A staked (boundary) survey physically marks the property corners and is more precise and more expensive. A survey location report shows the general location of improvements at a lower cost.

How much does a property survey cost?

Costs vary by property size, shape, and complexity, so we avoid quoting a figure. A staked survey generally costs more than a survey location report. A local surveyor can give you an accurate quote.

Do I need a survey for a home in a subdivision?

Not always. On a standard platted lot with clear records, many buyers skip the most detailed survey. If you plan to build a fence or addition, the added precision is often worth it.
