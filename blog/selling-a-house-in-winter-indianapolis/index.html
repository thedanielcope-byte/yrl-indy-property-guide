Selling a House in Winter in Indianapolis: Is It Worth It?

What the Central Indiana numbers say about listing in December, January and February, who should list now and who might wait, and how to get a house ready when it’s cold and the sun sets around 5:20.

Selling a Home Winter 2026 Central Indiana

✔ Reviewed by Janet Giles-Schultz, Principal Broker · MIBOR member · Updated November 2026

Selling a Home November 2026 By Daniel Cope, Real Estate Broker — Your Realty Link ~8 min read

Selling in Winter at a Glance

Should you sell your house in winter in Indianapolis? If you have a reason to move, it can work well. Far fewer homes hit the market (1,482 new listings across the metro in December 2025, versus 3,764 in April 2026), and homes still sell: 2,414 single-family homes closed in December 2025 at 97.5% of asking price, per MIBOR. The trade-offs: fewer buyers, slower sales (a median of 41 days on market in December versus 15 in June) and lower median prices than in summer.

Most sellers have heard the same advice: wait for spring. Sometimes that’s right. But a lot of Central Indiana homeowners don’t have the luxury of waiting, because of a job change, a new home that’s already under construction, or two mortgages they don’t want to carry until April. Here’s what the numbers say about selling a house in winter in Indianapolis, who it works for, and how to set your home up to sell when the yard is brown and the sun sets before dinner.

Winter vs. Spring in Indianapolis: What the Numbers Show

We pulled two sources: Realtor.com’s monthly listing data for the Indianapolis-Carmel-Anderson metro, and MIBOR’s monthly reports on single-family closed sales across its 17-county Central Indiana area.

Two things jump out. First, supply drops hard in winter: about 60% fewer new listings came on the market in December 2025 than in April 2026, and active listings fell about 26% from October 2025 (5,910) to February 2026 (4,356). Second, homes still sell in winter, just more slowly and with a little more negotiating: 2,414 single-family homes closed across MIBOR’s area in December 2025, at a median of 41 days on market and 97.5% of asking price, compared with 15 days and 98.7% in June 2026. Winter rewards the homes that are priced and presented well and is hard on the ones that miss.

The Upside of a Winter Listing

Less competition

With fewer new listings each month, a buyer touring in January has fewer homes to compare yours against. In spring, your house is one of thousands that came on in the same few weeks.

Serious buyers

People don’t usually tour homes in the snow for fun. In our experience, winter buyers tend to be on a deadline: a job transfer, a lease ending, a new-construction closing date that slipped, or a growing family. That often means fewer casual showings and more focused ones.

A head start on the spring wave

New listings climbed from 2,060 in January 2026 to 3,108 in March and 3,764 in April. Listing in late winter lets you meet early-spring buyers before most of your competition shows up.

The Downsides

  • Fewer buyers overall. Holidays, weather and school calendars thin the buyer pool, so a home that would draw several offers in May might draw one or two in January.
  • Slower sales. The median home that sold in January 2026 spent 50 days on the market, versus 15 in June 2026 (MIBOR), and overpriced homes sit the longest.
  • Lower median prices. MIBOR’s median single-family price was $310,000 in December 2025 and $300,000 in January 2026, versus $330,000 in June 2026. Part of that is likely mix, since more larger, higher-priced homes tend to sell in spring and summer, but don’t expect summer pricing in January.
  • More competition than last winter. There were 6,869 active listings across the metro in September 2026, about 20% more than the 5,730 a year earlier, and the median listing had been on the market 53 days, up from 47. Buyers going into this winter have more choices than they did last year.
  • Weather and curb appeal. Snow, slush and early darkness make showings, photos and some repairs harder.

Who Should List Now (and Who Might Wait)

A winter listing usually makes sense if:

  • You’re relocating or starting a new job on a set date (see our job relocation sale page).
  • You’ve already bought, or your new construction home has a closing date, and you don’t want to carry two payments.
  • Your home shows best inside: updated kitchen, good light, a finished basement.
  • You want to beat the spring rush and are willing to price to the current market.

Waiting for spring may make more sense if:

  • Your home’s best feature is outside, like a pool, landscaping or a big fenced yard.
  • You need exterior work, such as painting, concrete or roof repairs, that’s hard to do well in freezing weather.
  • You have no deadline and want the largest pool of buyers you can get.

How to Get a House Ready to Sell in Winter

Clear, safe walkways

Keep the driveway, sidewalk, porch steps and front door clear and salted every day you have showings. It’s the first impression and a safety issue for anyone touring.

Plan for the dark

The sun sets around 5:20 p.m. in Indianapolis in early December and doesn’t rise until about 8 a.m. near the winter solstice. Most weekday showings happen after dark, so replace burned-out bulbs, turn on exterior and porch lights, and leave lamps on before every showing.

Keep it warm

A cold house feels empty and raises questions about the furnace. Keep the thermostat at a normal living temperature during showings, and have a recent furnace service receipt ready for buyers to see.

Honest photos

Your listing photos should show the home as it is now. If you have good exterior photos from earlier in the year, they can help buyers picture the yard, but label them clearly as seasonal so nobody feels misled at the showing.

Go light on holiday decor

A wreath and a few lights are fine. Big displays, crowded trees and lots of personal items make rooms look smaller and photos look dated by January. Our home staging tips and list of staging mistakes apply year-round.

Protect a vacant house

If you’ve already moved out, keep every utility on. The American Red Cross recommends leaving the heat set no lower than 55°F when you’re away in cold weather, and a frozen pipe in an empty house can turn a sale into an insurance claim. Arrange snow removal, have someone check the house regularly, and ask your insurance agent whether your policy limits coverage on a vacant home. Our vacant home sale page has more.

Consider a pre-listing inspection

Finding a furnace, roof or plumbing problem before you list gives you time to fix it or price around it, instead of renegotiating after a buyer’s inspection. Our preferred inspector, HouseMaster Serving Heartland, does pre-listing inspections across the metro.

Pricing a Winter Listing

Pricing matters more in winter than any other time of year, because there are fewer buyers to absorb a mistake. Base the price on recent comparable sales, not an online estimate or what a neighbor got last May (here’s why a CMA beats a Zestimate). The first couple of weeks bring the most attention from buyers who’ve been waiting for something new. If you price high and plan to come down later, you risk sitting well past January’s 50-day median. For a broader look at timelines, see how long it takes to sell a house in Indianapolis.

Listing Over the Holidays

You don’t have to choose between selling and having a normal holiday. Many sellers stay on the market and simply block off showing times around Thanksgiving, Christmas and New Year’s. Plan the calendar too: lenders, title companies and county offices close on holidays, so a contract signed in mid-December may not close until January. And a closing on December 30 lands in a different tax year than one on January 4, so ask your tax professional whether the date matters for you.

Our Take

Selling a house in winter in Indianapolis isn’t a mistake. It’s a different market: fewer buyers, much less competition, and very little patience for overpricing. If you have a reason to move now, a well-prepared, well-priced winter listing can sell as quickly as a spring one did. If you have no deadline and your home shines in the sunshine, spring is still the busiest season. Either way, start with a realistic number. Frequently Asked Questions

Is it a bad idea to sell a house in winter in Indianapolis?

Not necessarily. There are fewer buyers, but there is also much less competition: about 60% fewer new listings came on the metro market in December 2025 (1,482) than in April 2026 (3,764), per Realtor.com, and 2,414 single-family homes still closed across MIBOR's 17-county area that month, at 97.5% of asking price. The key is pricing to the current market.

Should I sell my house now or wait until spring?

It depends on your timeline and your home. If you are relocating, have already bought, or have a new construction closing date, listing now can save you months of carrying costs. If your home's best features are outdoors or you need exterior repairs, and you have no deadline, spring gives you the largest pool of buyers.

What is the best month to sell a house in Indianapolis?

Spring and early summer are the busiest. In 2026, new listings across the Indianapolis metro peaked in April at 3,764 (Realtor.com), and homes that sold in June spent a median of just 15 days on the market (MIBOR). Spring also brings the most competition, so the best month for you depends on your timeline and how your home compares with what else is for sale.

Do houses sell for less in winter?

Median prices are usually lower. MIBOR reported a median single-family sale price of $310,000 in December 2025 and $300,000 in January 2026, compared with $330,000 in June 2026. Some of that reflects which homes sell in each season, and a well-priced home faces fewer competing listings in winter.

Should I take my house off the market over the holidays?

You don't have to. Many sellers stay on the market and block off showing times around Thanksgiving, Christmas and New Year's. Buyers searching in late December are usually serious. Keep in mind that lenders and title companies close on holidays, so a December contract may close in January.

How do I protect a vacant house I'm selling in winter?

Keep all utilities on, and keep the heat set no lower than 55 degrees, which is what the American Red Cross recommends when a home is empty in cold weather. Arrange snow removal, have someone check the house regularly, and ask your insurance agent whether your policy limits coverage on a vacant home.
