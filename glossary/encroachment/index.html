What Is an Encroachment?

When a fence, shed, or driveway crosses a line it isn't supposed to.

📘 Real Estate Term 📍 Indiana 🔑 Buyers & Sellers

Quick Answer

An encroachment means something physical, like a fence or a shed, sits over the property line. A survey usually reveals it, and while it's not always a dealbreaker, it typically needs to be addressed before closing.

What it is
A physical structure crossing a line
Usually found by
A property survey
Common examples
Fences, sheds, driveways, overhangs
Not the same as
An easement, which is a use right

How Encroachments Get Discovered

Most encroachments turn up on a current survey, which compares the actual footprint of structures against the recorded property lines. A visual walkthrough often can't catch a fence that's a foot or two over the line, especially on older lots where boundaries were staked out decades ago.

Encroachments can also be a symptom of an outdated survey being relied on, which is one reason buyers on older or irregular lots sometimes order a fresh one.

How They Get Resolved

Options range from a simple agreement between neighbors acknowledging the situation, to physically moving the structure, to a formal boundary line adjustment recorded with the county. In many cases, a minor encroachment like a foot of fence doesn't stop a sale, as long as it's disclosed and the buyer accepts it.

An encumbrance more broadly includes encroachments along with other claims that can affect a property's title or use.

Frequently Asked Questions — Encroachment

Does an encroachment kill a home sale?

Not usually. Minor encroachments are often resolved through disclosure, a written agreement, or a boundary adjustment rather than stopping the sale.

Who has to fix an encroachment?

It depends on the situation and what the parties agree to; sometimes the structure is moved, sometimes it's addressed through a recorded agreement instead.

Can a neighbor's shed on my land become theirs over time?

Indiana law recognizes adverse possession in limited circumstances, but it's fact-specific and worth discussing with a real estate attorney rather than assuming.

How do I know if I have an encroachment?

A current survey is the most reliable way to confirm whether a structure crosses a property line.
